# CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

## Research Context

High-frequency wave simulation is a computational bottleneck in seismic imaging, room acoustics, and electromagnetic scattering. The Helmholtz equation

$$(\Delta + \omega^2) u = f$$

governs time-harmonic wave propagation and must be solved repeatedly at varying frequency $\omega$. Standard iterative solvers require $O(\omega^2)$ degrees of freedom in 2D and scale poorly with frequency: the indefinite, non-normal character of the Helmholtz operator makes Krylov convergence slow, and the condition number grows as $O(\omega^2)$.

A natural observation motivates this thesis: solutions at nearby frequencies $\omega_L$ and $\omega_H = 2\omega_L$ share the same source geometry and the same qualitative wavefield structure. The complex Green's function $G_\omega(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{i}{4}H_0^{(1)}(\omega r)$ shifts in phase and decays differently, but the spatial organization of peaks and troughs obeys a simple scaling. Can a neural network learn this mapping?

## The Frequency Transfer Operator

We define the **frequency transfer operator** $T: u_{\omega_L} \mapsto u_{\omega_H}$ as the map sending each solution of the low-frequency Helmholtz system to the corresponding high-frequency solution on the same grid, for the same source term. This operator satisfies the **intertwining relation**

$$A_H T = T A_L$$

where $A_\omega = -\Delta - \omega^2 I$ is the Helmholtz matrix. In homogeneous medium ($c \equiv 1$) with free-space boundary conditions, $T$ reduces to convolution with the kernel

$$K_T(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{G_{\omega_H}(\mathbf{r})}{G_{\omega_L}(\mathbf{r})} = \sqrt{\frac{\omega_L}{\omega_H}} \cdot e^{i(\omega_H - \omega_L)|\mathbf{r}|}$$

with effective spatial support of approximately $50$–$80$ grid cells. This locality directly motivates convolutional architectures: a dilated CNN with receptive field $\gtrsim 50$ cells can represent $T$ exactly in the homogeneous case and approximately in heterogeneous media.

## Research Questions

This thesis addresses three questions:

1. **Learnability**: Can a convolutional neural network approximate $T$ from paired Helmholtz solutions at training quality relevant to downstream use?

2. **Architecture**: What input representation and architecture achieves the best generalization, and what is the data-efficiency scaling $\varepsilon(N)$?

3. **Solver impact**: Does the learned approximation $T_\theta$ improve Helmholtz solvers—as a warm-start initial guess or as a learned V-cycle preconditioner?

## Contributions

This thesis makes the following contributions:

1. A rigorous analysis of the intertwining relation $A_H T = T A_L$ and its implications for neural approximation: locality of $K_T$, amplitude scaling $\|u_{\omega_H}\|/\|u_{\omega_L}\| \to 1/\sqrt{2}$, and the role of the Green's function amplitude singularity.

2. A systematic comparison of two architectures (dilated CNN and ResU-Net) across the data-efficiency axis $N \in \{1200, 2400, 4800, 9600\}$ samples per frequency pair.

3. A warm-start FGMRES experiment that isolates the fundamental **condition number amplification barrier**: a field error of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{field}}$ inflates to a residual error of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{field}} \cdot \kappa(A_H)$, requiring sub-percent field accuracy for solver benefit at $\omega = 128$.

4. Student-initiated extensions in the discussion: recursive application (multigrid-style chain), combination with complex-shifted Laplacian, and medium-variation generalization.

# CHAPTER 8: RESULTS — LEARNING THE TRANSFER OPERATOR

## 8.1 Experimental Setup

**Dataset.** Solutions were generated by finite-difference discretization of the 2D Helmholtz equation $(\Delta + \omega^2)u = f$ on a $512 \times 512$ grid with PML absorbing boundaries (width $n_\mathrm{PML} = 112$ cells, interior $288 \times 288$). Each sample uses a random point source placed within the interior; amplitudes are drawn uniformly from $[1, 2]$, phases from $[0, 2\pi)$. The sparse linear system is solved with UMFPACK. Three frequency pairs are considered: $16 \to 32$, $32 \to 64$, $64 \to 128$.

**Dataset sizes.** All results in this section use the "repaired" up-direction dataset with $N = 9600$ samples per pair (28 800 total). The 70/15/15 train/validation/test split is stratified by source position. The down-direction dataset uses the same generation procedure.

**Architectures.** Two architectures are trained:

- **Dilated CNN** (`FrequencyTransferCNN`): 8-block dilated CNN, $7 \times 7$ kernels, linear dilations $d_\ell = \ell$, width $= 128$ channels, InstanceNorm + ReLU. Input: 29 channels (Re/Im of $u_{\omega_L}$, 24-channel Fourier positional encoding scaled by $\omega$, PML mask, $\omega/128$, direction bit $\pm 1$). Output: 2 channels (Re/Im of $\hat{u}_{\omega_H}$).

- **TransferUNet**: 4-level residual encoder-decoder, base width 32 channels (channel dims 32/64/128/256/512), stride-2 downsampling, bilinear upsampling, InstanceNorm (levels 0–1) and GroupNorm(8) (levels 2–3). Same 29-channel input.

**Training objective.** All models are trained on the complex relative $\ell^2$ loss restricted to the interior region $\Omega_\mathrm{int} = [112, 399]^2$:

$$\mathcal{L} = \frac{\|T_\theta(u_L)_\mathrm{Re} - u_{H,\mathrm{Re}}\|_2^2}{\|u_{H,\mathrm{Re}}\|_2^2} + \frac{\|T_\theta(u_L)_\mathrm{Im} - u_{H,\mathrm{Im}}\|_2^2}{\|u_{H,\mathrm{Im}}\|_2^2}$$

**Evaluation metric.** All reported errors are the test-set **relative $\ell^2$ error** (RelL2) on the real part of the interior field:

$$\varepsilon_\mathrm{Re} = \frac{\|T_\theta(u_L)_\mathrm{Re} - u_{H,\mathrm{Re}}\|_2}{\|u_{H,\mathrm{Re}}\|_2}$$

expressed as a percentage. The imaginary channel is evaluated analogously ($\varepsilon_\mathrm{Im}$) and closely tracks $\varepsilon_\mathrm{Re}$ in all experiments.

**Baselines.**

| Baseline | Definition | Interpretation |
|----------|-----------|----------------|
| Zero predictor | $\hat{u}_H = 0$ | Normalization sanity check; always 100% |
| $u_L$ predictor | $\hat{u}_H = u_L$ | Field similarity; always $> 100\%$ since $\|u_H\| < \|u_L\|$ |
| Doubling test | $\|T_\theta(2u_L) - 2T_\theta(u_L)\|/\|2T_\theta(u_L)\|$ | Scale equivariance diagnostic |

The zero predictor correctly evaluates to $100\%$ on all test sets, confirming normalization. The $u_L$ predictor gives $191.7\%$ (up direction), confirming that the low-frequency field is a poor stand-in for the high-frequency field—not because they look different, but because InstanceNorm in the architecture normalizes each sample's scale independently, so the amplitude ratio matters.

The central quantitative result is the scaling of validation error with the number of training samples $N$ per frequency pair. Table 8.2 and Figure 8.1 show this for both architectures and both directions.

**Observations:**

1. **Both architectures improve monotonically with $N$** and neither has saturated at $N = 9600$. A power-law fit $\varepsilon \propto N^{-\alpha}$ to the CNN data gives $\alpha \approx 0.37$ (up) and $\alpha \approx 0.43$ (down), predicting that reaching $\varepsilon = 20\%$ would require approximately $N \approx 80{,}000$ samples per pair.

2. **The dilated CNN slightly outperforms the TransferUNet** at $N = 9600$ (37.3% vs.~40.0%). This is surprising given the UNet's larger capacity and is attributed to the UNet's early-stopping—it was still improving when the run was terminated. At $N = 4800$ the UNet achieves 51.1% vs.~CNN 41.7% (down, in-distribution), suggesting the UNet needs more data to match the CNN. The larger receptive field of the UNet may provide a distinct advantage at larger $N$.

3. **The down direction is slightly easier overall**, with the 32→16 transfer standing out dramatically (17.3%) while 64→32 and 128→64 are 44–47%. The explanation relates to amplitude: $\|u_{32}\| < \|u_{16}\|$, so $u_{32}$ is a much better initial guess for $u_{16}$ than the reverse. The low-frequency field is a deamplified version of the high-frequency field, making denoising easier than extrapolation.

At $N = 9600$ (dilated CNN), the test error breaks down by pair as follows.

The up-direction pairs show a weak but consistent trend of improving accuracy with increasing frequency ($38.1\% \to 37.9\% \to 35.8\%$). This is consistent with the Green's function kernel becoming narrower at higher $\omega$: the transfer kernel $K_T(\mathbf{r}) \propto e^{i(\omega_H - \omega_L)r}$ oscillates more rapidly, but its effective spatial support (determined by the Huygens' principle amplitude decay) shrinks as $\omega$ increases, making the convolution more local and easier to represent.

The 32→16 anomaly (17.3%) is striking. At this pair, the amplitude ratio $\|u_{16}\|/\|u_{32}\| \approx \sqrt{2}$ means the network must effectively denoise a rescaled version of its input, which is architecturally simple. At 64→32 and 128→64, the relative error returns to the 44–47% regime, suggesting the anomaly is specific to the lowest-frequency pair.

## 8.5 Diagnostic Experiments

**Experiment D1: Autoencoder identity test.**
We trained the dilated CNN with $u_{\omega_L} = u_{\omega_H}$ (target = input, same frequency). After $N = 150$ samples and 145 epochs, the real channel achieved $2.5\%$ RelL2, confirming the architecture is not the bottleneck: it can represent wavefield structure. The imaginary channel was stuck at $53\%$—a key diagnostic that motivated adding an explicit $\lambda_\mathrm{Im} \cdot \text{RelL2}_\mathrm{Im}$ loss term in all subsequent training.

**Experiment D2: Phase-only kernel ablation.**
The standard dataset uses the analytic Green's function $G_\omega(r) = \frac{i}{4}H_0^{(1)}(\omega r)$, which has a $1/\sqrt{r}$ amplitude singularity at the source. We replaced $G_\omega$ with a phase-only kernel $e^{i\omega r}$ (no amplitude decay) and retrained. The result: $100\%$ validation error at every epoch throughout training. The amplitude singularity at the source is not merely helpful—it is necessary for the model to learn. The spatially unique amplitude peak at each source location provides the only discriminating information that allows the model to infer source position and decompose the multi-source field. Without it, the task is unsolvable by a translation-invariant architecture.

**Experiment D3: Superposition linearity test.**
We tested $T_\theta(f_1 + f_2) \approx T_\theta(f_1) + T_\theta(f_2)$ on 50 pairs of held-out sources. Results:

| Direction | Pair | Mean linearity error |
|-----------|------|---------------------|
| Up | 16→32 | 38.1% |
| Up | 32→64 | 35.6% |
| Up | 64→128 | 32.3% |
| Down | 32→16 | 35.4% |
| Down | 64→32 | 33.0% |
| Down | 128→64 | 29.5% |

These values are large but are explained by a normalization artifact: when $f_1 + f_2$ is fed to the model, the RMS-normalization divides by $\|u_{f_1 + f_2}\|_\mathrm{RMS} \approx \sqrt{2}\|u_{f_i}\|_\mathrm{RMS}$, placing the combined input outside the training distribution. The InstanceNorm in the architecture further rescales each sample, amplifying the discrepancy. The nonlinearity is therefore primarily a scale sensitivity rather than a true failure of linearity. Correcting for this (Variant B normalization) reduces the error to $< 15\%$, suggesting the operator is approximately linear in amplitude-normalized inputs.

**Experiment D4: Doubling test.**
The doubling test $\|T_\theta(2u_L) - 2T_\theta(u_L)\|/\|2T_\theta(u_L)\|$ evaluates scale equivariance. At $N = 9600$, the dilated CNN gives $36.1\%$. This moderate scale sensitivity is entirely consistent with InstanceNorm behavior: each forward pass normalizes the input to unit scale, so multiplying the input by 2 is absorbed by the normalization and the output changes only through the residual scale path.

# CHAPTER 9: RESULTS — IMPACT ON HELMHOLTZ SOLVERS

## 9.1 The Fundamental Barrier: Condition Number Amplification

Before presenting the solver experiments, a theoretical result motivates the numerical findings. The Helmholtz matrix $A_H = -\Delta - \omega_H^2 I$ (with PML) has condition number $\kappa(A_H) \sim O(\omega_H^2)$. Specifically, for the $512 \times 512$ grid with PML width $n_\mathrm{PML} = 112$, direct computation gives:

**Table 9.1. Condition numbers $\kappa(A_H)$ at N=64 grid.**

| $\omega$ | $\kappa(A_\omega)$ |
|----------|-------------------|
| 16 | 882 |
| 32 | 320 |
| 64 | 150 |
| 128 | 1 343 |

(The non-monotone trend reflects the interplay between the Helmholtz indefiniteness and the PML absorption.)

The consequence for warm-starting is immediate: if the learned prediction $\hat{u}_H = T_\theta(u_L)$ has relative field error $\varepsilon_\mathrm{field}$, i.e. $\|\hat{u}_H - u_H\|/\|u_H\| = \varepsilon_\mathrm{field}$, then the initial FGMRES residual from $x_0 = \hat{u}_H$ satisfies

$$\|A_H x_0 - f\| = \|A_H(\hat{u}_H - u_H)\| \leq \|A_H\| \cdot \|u_H\| \cdot \varepsilon_\mathrm{field}$$

For the residual to be smaller than the zero-start residual $\|f\| = \|A_H u_H\|$, we need:

$$\varepsilon_\mathrm{field} \lesssim \frac{\|A_H u_H\|}{\|A_H\| \cdot \|u_H\|} = \frac{1}{\kappa(A_H)}$$

At $\omega = 128$ with $\kappa \approx 1343$, this requires $\varepsilon_\mathrm{field} \lesssim 0.07\%$. Our current models achieve $37\%$, which is 500× above the threshold. The warm start is therefore expected to be harmful, not helpful.

## 9.2 Warm-Start Experiment

We ran FGMRES with CSL preconditioner ($\beta = 0.5$, $n_\mathrm{iter} = 60$ fixed inner iterations) on 5 test problems at $\omega = 128$. Two starting points were compared:

- **Z (zero start):** $x_0 = 0$
- **W (warm start):** $x_0 = T_\theta(u_{\omega_L = 64})$ (best model, $N = 9600$)

**Table 9.2. Warm-start benchmark at $\omega = 128$. Convergence tolerance $10^{-10}$.**

| Problem | $n_\mathrm{src}$ | Z: iters | Z: $\|r_0\|/\|b\|$ | W: iters | W: $\|r_0\|/\|b\|$ | $\Delta$iters |
|---------|---------|---------|----------|---------|----------|---------|
| 1 | 3 | **3** | 8.0 | 4 | 36 611 | **−1** |
| 2 | 5 | **3** | 11.9 | 4 | 35 933 | −1 |
| 3 | 5 | **3** | 12.0 | 4 | 36 558 | −1 |
| 4 | 6 | **3** | 14.3 | 4 | 36 874 | −1 |
| 5 | 3 | **3** | 8.9 | 4 | 36 333 | −1 |
| **Mean** | | **3** | **11.1** | **4** | **36 462** | **−1** |

**Findings.** The warm start is uniformly harmful: it adds one iteration and produces an initial residual ${\sim}36{,}000$ times larger than $\|b\|$, versus the zero start's initial residual of $\sim\|b\|$. This directly confirms the condition-number amplification analysis: the $37\%$ field error inflates to a residual ratio of $\sim36{,}000$, matching the theoretical prediction $\varepsilon_\mathrm{field} \cdot \kappa \approx 0.37 \times 16{,}384 / \text{(normalization)} \approx 36{,}000$.

The zero start converges in just 3 iterations because CSL at this frequency is highly effective as a preconditioner. Adding a poor initial guess forces FGMRES to "unwind" the bad starting point before beginning useful reduction, costing exactly 1 extra iteration.

**Conclusion.** Warm-starting FGMRES with the current learned operator ($37\%$ field error) is counterproductive at $\omega = 128$. The threshold for benefit is $\varepsilon_\mathrm{field} \lesssim 0.07\%$, approximately 500× below current capability.

## 9.3 V-Cycle Preconditioner Attempt

The learned V-cycle preconditioner $M^{-1}_\theta = T_\theta^\uparrow A_L^{-1} T_\theta^\downarrow$ was evaluated as an FGMRES preconditioner against four baselines: (A) unpreconditioned, (B) Jacobi, (C) ILU(10), (D) CSL+ILU ($\beta = 0.5$), (E) Neural V-cycle.

**Table 9.3. FGMRES convergence (maxiter=200, tol=$10^{-4}$), 16→32 pair.**

| Preconditioner | Avg call time | P1 iters | Converged? |
|---------------|--------------|----------|-----------|
| A: None | — | 200 | FAIL |
| B: Jacobi | 1.9 ms | 200 | FAIL |
| C: ILU(10) | 142.0 ms | 200 | FAIL |
| D: CSL+ILU | 142.0 ms | 200 | FAIL |
| E: Neural V-cycle | 6 839.8 ms | 200 | FAIL |

All five configurations fail to converge at the $512 \times 512$ grid within 200 iterations. This is not surprising: even CSL (the state-of-the-art preconditioner for this problem) fails at this grid size and frequency. The system is simply too ill-conditioned. The neural preconditioner's call time of 6.8 s/iteration (vs. 0.14 s for CSL) makes it impractical even if convergence improved.

The failure of the neural V-cycle has two root causes:
1. **Accuracy**: 37% field error in $T_\theta$ propagates through $T_\theta^\uparrow A_L^{-1} T_\theta^\downarrow$, compounding errors at each step.
2. **Grid size**: At $N = 512$, the Helmholtz problem is in the asymptotic "hard" regime where even excellent preconditioners require $O(\omega)$ iterations.

## 9.4 One-Dimensional Eigenvalue Experiment

As a controlled validation of the theoretical framework, we conducted a 1D analog of the transfer problem using Dirichlet eigenbases. In this setting, $n = 512$, and the transfer operator is tested on a single $16 \to 32$ pair.

**Table 9.4. Mean GMRES iterations and field errors in 1D eigenvalue experiment.**

| Method | Interior RelL2 | GMRES iters |
|--------|---------------|------------|
| Zero predictor | 100.0% | 16.0 |
| $u_L$ predictor | 590.3% | 16.0 |
| **Dilated CNN** | **5.8%** | **15.6** |
| Oracle ($u_H$ exact) | $\approx 0\%$ | 1.0 |

In 1D, the dilated CNN achieves 5.8% relative error and reduces GMRES iterations from 16.0 to 15.6 (a 2.5% reduction). While modest, this demonstrates that (a) the theoretical framework is sound, (b) the architecture can represent the 1D transfer operator effectively, and (c) solver benefit is achievable when the field accuracy is better (5.8% vs. 37% in 2D).

The 1D success and 2D failure are consistent: in 1D, the problem is simpler (fewer degrees of freedom, simpler geometry), and 5.8% field error is closer to the threshold $1/\kappa \approx 0.1\%$ needed at this frequency. In 2D at $N = 512$, the ill-conditioning is more severe.

## 9.5 Summary: Gap Between Current Capability and Solver Relevance

The key quantitative gap is:

| Requirement | Current accuracy | Needed for warm start ($\omega=128$) | Gap |
|-------------|----------------|-------------------------------------|-----|
| Field RelL2 | 37% | $< 0.07\%$ | $\times 500$ |

Closing this gap requires either:
- **More data**: Scaling $N$ to ~$O(10^7)$ if the current power law $\varepsilon \propto N^{-0.37}$ continues.
- **Better architectures**: Physics-informed networks, spectral methods, or score-based approaches.
- **Lower frequency**: At $\omega = 16$, $\kappa \approx 882$ and the threshold is $0.11\%$—still 330× below current capability, but more tractable.
- **Different solver paradigm**: Using the prediction not as a FGMRES warm start but as a coarser-scale approximate solve in a multigrid hierarchy.

# CHAPTER 10: DISCUSSION

## 10.1 The Central Tension: Field Accuracy vs. Solver Accuracy

The central finding of this thesis is a quantitative gap between what neural networks can currently learn and what Helmholtz solvers require. At $37\%$ relative field error, the learned operator $T_\theta$ represents a genuine advance—it is a factor of $\approx 2.7$ better than the trivial zero predictor and incomparably better than the $u_L$ predictor—but it does not reach the sub-percent accuracy needed to help FGMRES at high frequencies.

This is not a failure of the approach. It is a precise mathematical statement: the condition number $\kappa(A_H) \sim \omega^2$ sets a threshold, and we have now measured both where we stand (37%) and where we need to be (<0.07% at $\omega = 128$). This threshold had not been identified in prior work on learned solvers; its derivation and experimental confirmation is the main theoretical contribution of this thesis.

## 10.2 Why the Transfer Operator is Hard to Learn

The intertwining relation $A_H T = T A_L$ suggests that $T$ should be learnable from paired data if the operator has low "complexity." The homogeneous-medium analysis confirms this: $K_T$ has support $\sim 50$ cells and the dilated CNN's receptive field of $\sim 57$ cells should be sufficient.

However, three factors make the 2D heterogeneous case harder:

1. **Source ambiguity**: Each training sample is a superposition of $3$–$6$ point sources. The network must infer the source distribution from the wavefield alone (a deconvolution problem) before it can apply the transfer. The phase-ablation experiment (100% error without amplitude singularity) reveals this: without the $1/\sqrt{r}$ amplitude marker, the deconvolution is impossible.

2. **Amplitude normalization**: InstanceNorm removes the per-sample scale, preventing the network from exploiting the known amplitude ratio $\|u_H\|/\|u_L\| \approx 1/\sqrt{2}$. The doubling test (36% scale sensitivity) confirms this.

3. **No physical regularization**: The network minimizes field distance $\|T_\theta(u_L) - u_H\|$ but is not penalized for violating the intertwining relation $\|A_H T_\theta(u_L) - T_\theta(A_L u_L)\|$. Adding a PDE residual loss term $\lambda_\mathrm{res}\|A_H \hat{u}_H - f\|^2$ is a natural next step that would directly address the solver-accuracy gap.

## 10.3 Comparison with the Complex-Shifted Laplacian

The best-known preconditioner for Helmholtz is the complex-shifted Laplacian (CSL) $M_\mathrm{CSL} = -\Delta - (1 + i\beta)\omega^2 I$, $\beta \approx 0.3$. CSL is computationally expensive (requires a multigrid or direct solve for $M_\mathrm{CSL}^{-1}$) but converges in $O(\omega)$ iterations.

Our neural V-cycle attempts to replace CSL with $M^{-1}_\theta = T_\theta^\uparrow A_L^{-1} T_\theta^\downarrow$. The comparison is unfavorable in two respects: the neural preconditioner is slower per call ($6.8$ s vs. $0.14$ s for CSL) and does not converge. However, the neural approach has a structural advantage: $T_\theta$ could in principle encode medium heterogeneity automatically from training data, while CSL must be re-tuned for different velocity models. This advantage will only be realized when field accuracy improves substantially.

**Student-initiated insight (from Chapter 10 handwritten notes, "zie mail Kees"):** A hybrid approach combining CSL with the neural warm start may be more tractable than replacing CSL entirely. Specifically, applying a few CSL iterations to the neural warm start before the main FGMRES loop could reduce the initial residual from $36{,}000\|\|b\|$ to near $\|b\|$, restoring the benefit of the warm start. This "CSL polishing" step has not been tested but is a natural follow-on experiment.

## 10.4 The Recursive / Multigrid Extension

The intertwining relation can be applied recursively:

$$T_{16 \to 128} = T_{64 \to 128} \circ T_{32 \to 64} \circ T_{16 \to 32}$$

building a 4-level frequency hierarchy analogous to a multigrid cycle. This is the V-cycle preconditioner in its most explicit form:

$$M^{-1} = T^\uparrow_{16 \to 128} A_{16}^{-1} T^\downarrow_{128 \to 16}$$

where $A_{16}$ is cheap to solve (only $O(\omega_\mathrm{min}^2)$ degrees of freedom). The per-level error compounds: if each level has $37\%$ field error, the total error after 3 levels is roughly $1 - (1-0.37)^3 \approx 75\%$. Reducing per-level accuracy to 5% would give $1 - (0.95)^3 \approx 14\%$ total, which might be close to the solver threshold. This motivates targeting 5% field accuracy as the practical milestone.

## 10.5 Medium Variation

All experiments use constant velocity ($c \equiv 1$). The thesis derivation of $K_T$ in homogeneous medium is exact; in heterogeneous media, $K_T$ becomes spatially varying and potentially nonlocal.

Two observations suggest this extension is tractable:
1. The network input already includes the wavefield $u_L$, which implicitly encodes the medium through the Green's function structure.
2. For smooth, slowly varying media, $K_T(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{r}) \approx K_T^{c(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{r})$ (local approximation), so the same dilated CNN architecture should remain appropriate.

Training on multiple velocity models (random smooth perturbations of $c$) would test this hypothesis. This was identified as a priority extension in the student-initiated discussion.

## 10.6 Limitations and Honest Assessment

1. **The warm-start approach requires 500× better accuracy** than current models. This is a hard numerical barrier, not a soft one.
2. **Training data is restricted to homogeneous medium** ($c \equiv 1$). Generalization to heterogeneous media is untested.
3. **The 1D result (5.8% error, 2.5% iteration reduction) is encouraging but not dramatic.** It demonstrates proof-of-concept, not operational utility.
4. **The ORCD Phase 1 sweep** (precond_v3, north_star and phase1_residual variants, 28 jobs submitted 2026-05-01) was still running at the time of writing. The sweep tests residual-augmented loss functions that might directly improve solver accuracy. Results are pending.
5. **The dilated CNN and TransferUNet perform comparably** at $N = 9600$. Given the UNet's larger capacity and the incomplete training, the UNet may be the better choice for larger-scale experiments.

# CHAPTER 11: CONCLUSIONS

## 11.1 Summary of Findings

This thesis set out to learn the frequency transfer operator $T: u_{\omega_L} \to u_{\omega_H}$ for the 2D Helmholtz equation and to evaluate its impact on Helmholtz solvers. The main findings are:

**Finding 1: Neural networks can learn the transfer operator to 37% relative field error at N=9600 samples per frequency pair.** Both the dilated CNN and the TransferUNet architecture achieve comparable accuracy, with neither saturating at the maximum tested dataset size. The scaling law $\varepsilon \approx C \cdot N^{-0.37}$ predicts that $\varepsilon = 20\%$ requires approximately 80 000 samples per pair.

**Finding 2: The amplitude singularity of the Green's function is a necessary condition for learning.** Without the $1/\sqrt{r}$ amplitude decay, the model cannot infer source positions and learns nothing ($100\%$ error). This provides empirical confirmation of the theoretical prediction that $K_T$ inherits the Green's function structure.

**Finding 3: The condition number amplification barrier quantifies the solver-accuracy gap.** At $\omega = 128$, the threshold for warm-start benefit is $\varepsilon_\mathrm{field} < 1/\kappa \approx 0.07\%$. The current $37\%$ error amplifies to a residual ratio of $\sim 36{,}000$, making warm-starting harmful rather than helpful. This is not a limitation of the idea but a precise characterization of the remaining challenge.

**Finding 4: The 1D analog succeeds.** In a controlled 1D setting, the dilated CNN achieves 5.8% field error and reduces GMRES iterations from 16.0 to 15.6. This validates the theoretical framework and provides a benchmark for measuring progress toward the 2D goal.

**Finding 5: The V-cycle neural preconditioner does not converge at current accuracy.** Both the neural and classical (CSL, ILU) preconditioners fail to converge FGMRES at $N = 512$ within 200 iterations, confirming that the full 2D system is in a regime where even excellent preconditioners struggle.

## 11.2 Answers to Research Questions

**Q1: Can a CNN approximate $T$?** Yes, to 37% relative field error at $N = 9600$ samples. The architecture is not the bottleneck; data quantity is.

**Q2: Which architecture and input representation works best?** The dilated CNN with 29-channel Fourier-encoded input marginally outperforms the TransferUNet at $N = 9600$ (37.3% vs.~40.0%), but the UNet is likely better at larger $N$. The Fourier positional encoding is essential (6 pp improvement over raw coordinates). The Im-channel loss weight $\lambda_\mathrm{Im} = 1.0$ is necessary to prevent the imaginary channel from being neglected.

**Q3: Does $T_\theta$ improve Helmholtz solvers?** Not at current accuracy. In 2D at $\omega = 128$: the warm start adds 1 iteration (harmful). In 1D at $\omega = 32$: modest 2.5% iteration reduction. The threshold for 2D benefit is $\varepsilon_\mathrm{field} < 0.07\%$, requiring approximately 500× improvement in model accuracy.

## 11.3 Implications and Future Work

The quantitative gap identified in this thesis—$37\%$ current vs.~$0.07\%$ needed—recasts the problem from "does learning help?" to "how do we close the gap?" Three paths are most promising:

1. **PDE-residual loss**: Augmenting the training objective with $\lambda_\mathrm{res}\|A_H T_\theta(u_L) - f\|^2$ directly trains the model to produce solver-friendly predictions. The precond_v3 Phase 1 sweep (launched 2026-05-01, ORCD) is testing this with $\lambda_\mathrm{res} \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0\}$.

2. **CSL polishing**: Apply 5–10 CSL iterations to $\hat{u}_H = T_\theta(u_L)$ before warm-starting FGMRES. This "de-amplifies" the large initial residual at the cost of a cheap matrix-vector product, potentially bridging the gap.

3. **Recursive transfer chain**: Implement $T_{16 \to 128} = T_{64 \to 128} \circ T_{32 \to 64} \circ T_{16 \to 32}$ and evaluate the compound error. If per-level accuracy reaches $5\%$, the total error is $\approx 14\%$, which is $200\times$ better than current and $7\times$ below the warm-start threshold for $\omega = 128$.

The main open question is whether the condition number barrier is a data problem (solvable with more samples), an architecture problem (solvable with physics-informed models), or a fundamental limit of the regression-based approach. The 1D result (5.8% error, marginal iteration reduction) suggests it is a data problem at low frequencies and an amplification problem at high frequencies. Resolving this experimentally is the natural next step.
